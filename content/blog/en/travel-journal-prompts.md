Good travel journal prompts are questions you can answer standing outside the place, before you walk on. Not "describe the museum" but "which room would you send a friend to?" Not "how was dinner" but "name, dish, rating out of five." Below are 50 prompts built that way, grouped by when on a trip you will need them: before you leave, the first day, places, moments, people, mistakes, the last day, after you are home and a short set for travelling with kids. Most produce a number or one sentence. That is deliberate: a short answer written on the spot is worth more in five years than a paragraph written from memory at the hotel.

## What to write in a travel journal: how to use these prompts

Every prompt here fits into one of two kinds of entry. A **place** is somewhere you could go back to: a café, a viewpoint, a museum. Places get a rating out of five. A **moment** is something that happened once and cannot be revisited: a sudden downpour, a conversation on a tram, the minute you got lost. We leave moments unrated, because they were not a service you consumed. If this split is new to you, our guide on [how to keep a travel journal](/en/blog/how-to-keep-a-travel-journal/) explains the whole one-minute system; this list is the question bank that goes with it.

Practically: answers to the "places" prompts become place entries. Answers to the "moments" and "people" prompts become moment entries. Logistics answers go into the notes of the place they belong to, or into a moment tagged "tip". Do not try to answer all 50 on one trip. Two to four a day, chosen because they match what just happened, is the whole method.

The prompts work in a paper notebook, a notes app or a journal app. [VoyageLog](/), the app we make, is built around the place-and-moment split, so each answer is a title, a rating and one line of notes, but nothing below needs it.

## Before you leave and the first day

### Before the trip

1. Which three places are you booking this trip around? Name them; you will check back on the last day.
2. What do you expect the city to smell like at eight in the morning? One guess.
3. What are you most worried about: the language, the money, the trains, the food? One word.
4. What would make this trip a 5 out of 5? One sentence, written before you go.
5. What did you pack that you suspect you will not use? List it; check on the last day.

Expectations are the one thing you cannot write down after the fact, because the trip overwrites them. A guess about what Lisbon smells like, made from your sofa, is a small time capsule, and prompt 1 gives the last day something to measure against. These five take two minutes in total and they are the most reread part of many journals.

### First day

6. The first thing you ate after arriving: name, price, rating out of 5.
7. How long did it take from the airport or station to the bed? Minutes, and how you got there.
8. The first sentence in the local language you understood.
9. The first thing that looked different from home. One object, not a feeling.
10. Rate the first evening walk out of 5 and name the street.

The first day is when everything is still strange, and that strangeness fades within 48 hours. A note that the Prague tram was quieter than the bus at home, or that the walk from Barcelona Sants took 35 minutes with bags, is a detail you will not notice again on day three. These prompts catch it while you can still see it.

## Travel journal ideas for places: food, views, museums, neighbourhoods

11. The dish you would order again: place, dish, rating out of 5.
12. The dish you would not order again, and the one-word reason.
13. Coffee: where, how much, out of 5. Compare it to home in three words.
14. Best view so far: where you were standing, what time, and whether it was worth the climb.
15. In a museum, the one room you would send a friend straight to. Room name or number if you can.
16. How long was the museum queue, what time was it, and should you have booked a ticket?
17. Which neighbourhood would you stay in next time, and which would you skip?
18. A shop or market stall you will not find on any map: street name and what they sell.
19. The famous place that was still worth it: rating out of 5 and the time of day you went.
20. The famous place that was not worth it: rating, and what you would do instead.

Place prompts age well because they turn into recommendations. Three years on, a 5 next to Pizzarium in Rome with the note "worth the queue every time" is something you can send to a friend without rereading anything else. Prompt 16 is the one people skip and later regret: knowing that the Pantheon now charges admission, except on the first Sunday of the month, is the kind of line your next trip wants. Rate honestly and be stingy with 5s, or the numbers stop meaning anything.

## Moments, people and conversations

### Moments: things that happened once

21. What happened in the last hour that cannot happen again? One plain sentence.
22. Weather that changed the day: what it did and what you did about it.
23. The sound you will associate with this city: bells, scooters, gulls, a tram.
24. Something you saw a local do that you would never see at home.
25. The time you were briefly lost, and what you found there.
26. The minute you stopped feeling like a tourist, if it happened. Where were you standing?
27. One thing that made you laugh today, and who was there.

Moments are the actual story of a trip, and they are the entries that get lost when a journal is only ratings. The trick is to write them in the plainest words you have: "got caught in the rain coming down from Castelo de São Jorge, dried off in a bar with a football match on" is better than anything more literary, because it took twenty seconds and it got written. Tag them (rain, sunrise, lost) so a search for "rain" five years later brings back three cities at once.

### People and conversations

28. The name of one person who helped you today, and what they did.
29. A sentence someone said to you that you want to keep, as close to word for word as you can.
30. The best thing your travel companion did today, or the thing they loved that you did not.
31. The stranger you talked to longest: where, how long, what about.
32. One phrase in the local language that worked, and one that did not land.

Names disappear first. You will remember the waiter in Vienna who brought a second strudel for years, but his name will be gone by the flight home unless you wrote it down. Prompt 30 is for couples and friends: the thing your companion loved and you did not is a funnier and kinder record than a shared verdict. Keep these as moment entries, no rating, and let the photo do the describing.

## Mistakes and logistics worth remembering

33. The thing you should have booked in advance: name it, and the date you tried.
34. The wrong turn, train or bus: where it started and how much time it cost.
35. Roughly what a full day cost in local currency: food, transport, tickets.
36. What time did the thing close, open or leave that you got wrong?
37. What would you tell yourself at the start of today, in one sentence?
38. The item you wish you had packed, and the item you could have left at home.

These are the least romantic prompts and the most useful. A guidebook cannot tell you that tram 28 in Lisbon at nine is a crush and at seven is empty, or that the last ferry from Amalfi left earlier than the timetable at the hotel said. Your journal can, and the next person to need it is usually you. Attach logistics to the place entry they belong to, in the notes line, so they come up when you search the place and not just when you search "mistake".

## Travel journal questions for the last day and after you are home

### Last day

39. Rate the whole trip out of 5 and give one reason, before you reread any entries.
40. Go back to prompt 1. Which of the three places did you actually visit, and how did each rate?
41. The place you would walk to right now if you had one more hour.
42. Which neighbourhood or city gets the "stay here next time" tag?

The last day is the one chance to compare the trip you planned with the trip you had, which is why prompts 1 and 40 are a pair. The gap between the two is often the most interesting line in the whole journal: you booked the trip around the Belvedere and the thing you would walk back to is a bar with no name on Prague's Letná hill. Write 39 before you reread anything so your gut rating is not edited by your own notes.

### After you are home

43. A week later, which entry did you reread first? That is your real 5.
44. What are you still eating, drinking or doing differently because of the trip?
45. The one place you have already recommended to someone: who asked, and what did you say?
46. Your top three places from the trip, in order. Reorder them when you change your mind.

A journal nobody rereads is storage. These four force one rereading, which is usually enough to start the habit. Prompt 46 is a best-of list, and it is the thing you will actually send when a friend asks what to do in Barcelona. If your app has a trip map or a year-end look back, this is the week to open it; if you are on paper, flick through once and put a dot next to the entries that still land.

## Travel journal prompts for kids and family trips

47. The best thing you ate today, thumbs up or thumbs down, and would you eat it again?
48. Draw or photograph the strangest thing you saw today and give it a name.
49. How many steps, stairs, boats or trains today? The child chooses what to count.
50. Rate today out of five stars and say who gets the gold medal for the day.

With children the rule is that they own the answer and you own the pen. Ask the same one or two questions every evening so the comparison becomes the game: day one's strangest thing was a pigeon on a statue, day five's was a man selling roast chestnuts from a bicycle. Ratings and gold medals work at any age because they are a verdict, not an essay. On a longer trip, a family journal split by city makes a good thing to send the grandparents at the end, as a PDF or a card.

## Journal prompts for a vacation: picking the right one on the spot

Fifty prompts is a menu, not a checklist. The way to use it on a Tuesday in Rome is to notice what just happened and reach for the matching section: you ate something, so 11 or 12; it rained, so 22; you missed the last entry to the Vatican Museums, so 33 and 36. Two to four answers a day, each under a minute, gives you twenty or thirty entries for a week away. That is enough to hand a friend a recommendation list, reread as a story, and search by craving later, which is what [a travel journal is for](/en/blog/how-to-keep-a-travel-journal/).

If you would rather compare tools before you start, our roundup of the [best travel journal apps](/en/blog/best-travel-journal-apps/) covers everything from paper-like diaries to automatic route trackers. The prompts above work in all of them. The only requirement is that you answer while you are still standing there.
