# Best travel journal apps in 2026: 7 apps compared for real trips

The best travel journal app for you comes down to one question: do you want a private record you write yourself, or an automatic route map you share with other people? Those are two different products, and most of the disappointment in app reviews comes from picking one when you wanted the other. Below are seven apps that actually get used on trips, ranked, with a table so you can see at a glance which ones need an account, which work offline, and where your photos and notes end up.

## How the seven compare

| App | Account needed | Works offline | Auto GPS tracking | Where data lives | Free tier | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VoyageLog | No | Yes, fully | No | Your phone only | Limited number of trips, all core features | Private journaling of places and moments |
| Polarsteps | Yes | Tracking works offline, syncs later | Yes | Polarsteps cloud | Free | Automatic route map, sharing with family |
| Day One | Not on one device; yes for sync | Yes | No | Your device, plus encrypted cloud if you sync | Unlimited entries, one photo per entry, one device | Daily writing that includes trips |
| FindPenguins | Yes | Tracking and posts work offline, sync later | Yes, with flight detection | FindPenguins cloud | Free with fewer photos per post | Long trips, travel community |
| Journi | Yes | Historically a paid feature | No continuous track; map built from your posts and photo locations | Journi cloud | Free with limits; backup is paid | Printed photo books |
| Journey | Not to write locally; yes for sync | Yes | No | Your device, plus Google Drive or Journey Cloud if you sync | Free with paid extras and a membership | Cross-platform general diary |
| Penzu | Yes | Mobile app offers offline writing; web needs a connection | No | Penzu cloud | Unlimited entries; export and locking are paid | Text-only private diary on the web |

A note on Google Maps Timeline, which people often mention in this conversation: it is not a journal. It is a location log inside Google Maps, now stored on your device rather than Google's servers, with an optional encrypted backup to your Google account. It can tell you where you were on a Tuesday in March. It cannot tell you what you thought of the restaurant.

## 1. VoyageLog: for a private record that stays on your phone

VoyageLog is the app we build, so read this entry with that in mind. It exists because the other apps on this list are either social, cloud-first, or built for daily reflection rather than trips.

The model is simple. A trip holds entries. An entry is either a place (a café, a museum, a viewpoint) or a moment (a sunrise, a conversation, a wrong turn that turned out well). Each gets a title, optional photos or video, a note, tags and a rating out of five. Longer trips split by city, so ten days through Milan, Rome and Amalfi show up as three collapsible sections with their own dates and entries.

What you get around that core:

- A timeline of every trip in date order, with countries, length and tags on each card
- Search across titles, notes and tags, with filters for type, rating and tags
- A trip map, and one map of everywhere you have been
- Year in Travel, a month-by-month look back with a shareable card
- Best-of lists you reorder yourself: best pizza in Italy, best coffee in Lisbon
- City lists for places you hope to see, including ready-made seasonal lists such as Christmas markets and cherry blossom season
- Fifty-plus badges and short seasonal challenges

There is no account. There is no feed. Everything is stored on the device, and the app works entirely offline, which matters more than it sounds on a plane, in a metro tunnel or in a country where you are rationing roaming data. When you want a copy, you export it yourself: JSON for a full backup you can import on a new phone, Excel for a spreadsheet, ZIP with all photos and videos bundled, or PDF and image cards for a trip, a city or a best-of list.

The free version is a complete journal with a limited number of trips. Pro removes the limits, adds up to 50 photos per entry, advanced search filters, the full Year in Travel and exports of everything, and comes as either a yearly subscription or a one-time lifetime purchase.

Who should not pick it: anyone who wants a route drawn automatically while they walk, or who wants friends and family to follow along live. VoyageLog does not track your location in the background, and it has no followers. It is for remembering trips, not broadcasting or planning them.

## 2. Polarsteps: for the automatic route map

Polarsteps is the app most people picture when they hear travel tracker. You create an account, start a trip, and the Travel Tracker draws your route on a world map as you go. You add steps along the way with photos and text, and at the end you can order a printed book of the trip.

What it does well:

- The route map is genuinely satisfying, especially on a long multi-country trip
- Tracking keeps working offline and syncs when you reconnect
- Battery impact is modest; the app saves locations in batches rather than streaming them
- Family can follow a trip through a private link without their own account

What to know before you commit:

- You need an account to create trips, and your data lives in Polarsteps' cloud
- Background tracking has to be on for the map to fill in by itself; turn it off and you are placing steps by hand
- It is built around sharing. If nobody is going to look at your trip, a lot of the interface is pointing at the wrong goal

Pick Polarsteps if the map is the point and you like the idea of people following along. If you would rather keep the trip to yourself, read our piece on [Polarsteps alternatives](/en/blog/polarsteps-alternatives/).

## 3. Day One: for people who already journal every day

Day One is a general journaling app that happens to be excellent on trips. Entries carry location, weather and photos, and the writing experience is the best of anything on this list. If you already keep a daily journal, your travel entries probably belong here rather than in a separate app.

The free Basic tier gives unlimited journals and entries with end-to-end encryption, but limits you to one photo per entry and one device. Syncing across devices needs a paid plan and a Day One account. The paid tiers add more photos per entry, multi-device sync and, in the top tier, AI writing features.

Pick Day One if writing is the main activity and photos are secondary. Skip it if you want ratings, best-of lists, or a structure that separates one trip from the next without you building it by hand with tags.

## 4. FindPenguins: for long trips and a travel community

FindPenguins sits closest to Polarsteps. You create an account, turn on the tracker, and the app records your route with GPS and detects flights automatically. Posts are called footprints and can be created offline, then uploaded later. There is a community side where other travelers can follow and comment.

The free tier is ad-free with unlimited trips but allows fewer photos per footprint than Premium, which also adds longer videos and a discount on photo books.

Pick FindPenguins if you are away for months and want both the automatic route and a public audience. The same caveats as Polarsteps apply: account required, cloud storage, background tracking.

## 5. Journi: for turning a trip into a printed book

Journi's whole arc points toward the printed photo book at the end. You post memories with photos, the app groups them by time and location and builds an overview map from where your photos were taken, and the book is a few taps away. The design is warm and the books are nice.

The limits are in the free tier. Offline entries have historically been a Premium feature, and backup to Dropbox or Google Drive is explicitly Premium-only. An account is required.

Pick Journi if the book is the goal. Pick something else if you want to write offline for free or keep a local copy of your data.

## 6. Journey: for a cross-platform general diary

Journey is a long-running diary app with clients for Android, iOS, Mac, Windows and the web. It is pleasant to write in, supports photos and location on entries, and syncs through Google Drive app data or its own Journey Cloud service. You can write locally without signing in; syncing to another device needs a Journey account.

The base app is free with paid add-ons, and the Journey Cloud membership adds faster sync, end-to-end encryption and shared journals. It is a good choice for someone who moves between a laptop and a phone and wants the same diary on both.

Pick Journey if you are on Android and Windows and want one diary across everything. It is not trip-shaped, though, so you will organize travel with tags and date ranges rather than trips and cities.

## 7. Penzu: for text-first journaling on the web

Penzu is the oldest name here and the most text-focused. It began as a web diary and still feels like one: an account, a clean writing page, journals you can lock. The mobile app lists offline writing as a feature, and the free tier offers unlimited entries with cloud sync. PDF export, extra locking and entry recovery sit in the paid Pro tier.

Pick Penzu if you mostly write at a laptop in the evening and rarely attach photos. Pick something else if photos, maps or ratings matter to you.

## Which one should you actually install?

Here is the short version, by the thing you care about most.

- **Privacy and no account**: VoyageLog. Nothing leaves the phone unless you export it.
- **Automatic route map to share**: Polarsteps first, FindPenguins if you want the community and flight detection.
- **Daily writing habit that includes travel**: Day One.
- **A printed book at the end**: Journi, or Polarsteps' own book service.
- **One diary across phone, laptop and web**: Journey or Penzu.

Two questions to ask yourself before you decide. First, will you keep writing after the trip ends? Trackers get their value during the trip; journals get theirs years later, when you search for pizza and find the place in Rome you had forgotten. Second, who is the audience? If the answer is just you, every social feature is friction. If the answer is your parents following along from home, a tracker is the right tool and no amount of privacy will make a journal do that job.

Whichever app you choose, the habit matters more than the software. We wrote a short guide on [how to keep a travel journal](/en/blog/how-to-keep-a-travel-journal/) that works in any of these apps, built around capturing an entry in under a minute while you are still standing in the place.
