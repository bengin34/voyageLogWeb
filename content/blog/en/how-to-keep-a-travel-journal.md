# How to keep a travel journal that survives a real trip

The way to keep a travel journal is to make each entry so small that you can finish it standing on the pavement outside the place. A title, a rating out of five, one line of notes, maybe a photo. Under a minute. Most travel journals die on day three because the format asked for a page a night, and by day three you are tired, the hotel wifi is bad, and the evening you were going to write about has already become two evenings. Below is a system that gets around that, then what to write, what to skip and how to make the journal worth rereading years later.

## Why most travel journals stop on day three

The classic travel diary is a long entry written at the end of the day. It is a beautiful idea and a terrible system, for three reasons.

- **It depends on energy you will not have.** The day you most want to record is the day you walked 14 kilometres across Rome. That is also the day you fall asleep with the notebook on your chest.
- **It writes from memory, and memory is already blurring.** By ten at night the three churches you saw have merged. The specific thing that made the second one special is gone.
- **It has no structure for finding anything later.** Six months on, you want the name of the tapas bar in Barcelona. It is in a paragraph somewhere in the middle of day four.

The fix is to move the writing to the moment itself and to shrink it until it fits there.

## The one-minute capture system

The system works in a notebook, in a notes app or in a journal app built around it.

### 1. Capture while you are still standing there

Write the entry at the table, on the bridge, in the museum lobby, before you move on. Not at dinner, not at the hotel. The entry is not a reflection on the place; it is a note from inside it. This single habit does more for a travel journal than everything else combined, because the detail that makes an entry worth rereading is the one you only have for about ten minutes.

### 2. Decide: is this a place or a moment?

Every entry is one of two things.

- A **place** is somewhere you can go back to. Café Central in Vienna. The Belvedere. Pizzarium in Rome. A place has a location, and it can be rated, because you are judging whether it was worth it.
- A **moment** is something that happened and cannot be revisited. Sunrise on Charles Bridge with almost nobody there. Getting caught in the rain on the way down from Castelo de São Jorge in Lisbon. The tram in Prague where an old man corrected your pronunciation. Leave moments unrated: a moment was not a service you consumed. It just happened.

Separating these matters more than it looks. Years later, the places become recommendations you can hand to a friend, and the moments become the actual story of the trip. Mixing them makes both harder to find.

### 3. Rate places out of five, honestly

A rating is the fastest piece of writing there is. It takes two seconds, and it does the job of a paragraph. Three years on, a 5 next to Bar Cañete in Barcelona tells you everything: go back. A 3 next to a famous bakery tells you the queue was not worth it, without you having to write that sentence.

Be stingy. If everything is a 5, the number means nothing. A 5 is the place you would route a whole day around. A 4 is very good. A 3 is fine. Keep 1 and 2 for genuine disappointments; they are useful too, because the next time a guidebook recommends the place you will know.

### 4. Tag it with one or two words

Tags are how you find things later. Keep them boring and reusable: coffee, pizza, museum, viewpoint, bar, sunrise, beach. A tag you use once is useless; a tag you use forty times across five countries becomes a list of every viewpoint you have ever climbed to. Add the city as a tag too if your app does not do it for you.

### 5. Write one line of notes

One line. Not a description, not a review. The single thing you would say to a friend who asked. Lines like these age well:

- Book the morning slot, the stained glass is best before noon.
- Melange and apple strudel while the snow came down.
- Worth the queue every single time.
- Margherita only. Do not ask for anything else.
- Manageable weekday queues, Klimt room gets busy by eleven.

Notice that none of these describe the place. You have the photo for that. They record a judgment, a tip or a condition, which is what you will want from your past self.

### 6. Take one photo that is not the postcard

You will take the postcard photo anyway. For the journal, take the one that shows what it was like to be there: the menu board, the ticket, the view from your seat, the queue, the plate before you ate it. It attaches to the entry and fills in everything you did not write.

That is the whole system. Title, place or moment, rating, tag, one line, one photo. It takes less than a minute, which is the only reason it survives.

## What to write in a travel journal

With the system in place, these are the things worth capturing, in rough order of how much you will value them later.

### Judgments

Was it worth it? That is the question your future self is asking, and a rating plus one line answers it. Every place gets this.

### Conditions and logistics that would change the plan

The things you wish you had known that morning. In Lisbon, that tram 28 at nine in the morning is a crush and at seven is empty. In Vienna, that the Belvedere's weekday queues are fine. In Rome, that the Pantheon now needs a ticket. In Barcelona, that the towers of the Sagrada Família need a ticket that includes them, and the basic one does not. These lines are what make your journal more useful than a guidebook for the next person, which is usually you.

### Food, specifically

Food entries are the ones people search for most. Not because food matters most, but because you forget it fastest and want it back hardest. The name of the dish, the price if it surprised you, whether you would order it again. The croquetas and cold vermouth at the counter. The slice you ate standing up. One line each.

### Moments, in the plainest words you have

For moments, resist the urge to write well. Just say what happened. Freezing on Charles Bridge at seven, worth it for the empty view. Got lost in Alfama and found a tiny square with a man playing guitar. The temptation to make it literary is exactly what turns a thirty-second entry into a ten-minute one you skip.

### Numbers

How much the museum ticket cost. How long the train from Prague to Vienna took. What time the last ferry left. Numbers are cheap to write and oddly moving to reread.

## What to skip

- **The full itinerary.** You do not need to write that you woke up, had breakfast and went to the museum. The entries themselves are the itinerary, in order.
- **Descriptions of famous things.** The Trevi Fountain does not need your description. It needs your rating, and your note about whether seven in the morning was quiet.
- **Weather, unless it mattered.** The snow in Vienna mattered. Partly cloudy did not.
- **Anything you would be embarrassed to find a stranger reading.** A travel journal is not a confessional, and keeping those two separate keeps the travel journal fast.
- **Guilt.** If you miss a day, you miss a day. The system is designed so one gap does not become a reason to stop.

## Travel journal ideas for people who hate writing

Not everyone wants to write sentences, even short ones. All of these work with the same place-and-moment structure and need almost no words.

- **Running best-of lists.** Best coffee in Lisbon. Best pizza in Italy. Best viewpoint this year. Rank them as you go; reorder them when a new entry beats an old one.
- **A rating-only journal.** Title, rating, photo, nothing else. It is surprisingly complete.
- **One-tag entries.** The entry is the tag. Sunrise. Pizza. Bar. The photo does the rest.
- **City checklists.** Keep a list of cities you want to see and tick them off. Seasonal versions work well too: a [Christmas markets checklist](/en/blog/christmas-markets-europe-checklist/) that you work through over a few winters, or cherry blossom cities.
- **Photograph the paper.** Menus, tickets, receipts, the hand-drawn map the hotel gave you. Attach them to the entry and you have the day without writing a word.

## Choosing where to keep it

Paper has one great virtue: nothing about it needs charging. It also cannot be searched, cannot hold a photo without glue, and is in a drawer when you are standing in the place. A phone is always with you and finds things. ## How to reread it later

A journal nobody rereads is just storage. A few ways to make the rereading happen.

- **Search by craving.** The best way back into an old trip is a word: pizza, sunrise, Vienna. Search finds the entry, and the entry brings the day with it.
- **Build the lists.** After a trip, pull the 5s into a best-of list. Best three meals, best view. The list is what you will send to friends who ask what to do in Prague, and making it is a pleasant hour.
- **Look at the map.** Every place with a location, on one map of everywhere you have been, is a different kind of memory from the timeline. The gaps are as interesting as the clusters.
- **Do the year-end look back.** Once a year, scroll the whole year in order. Count the trips, cities and countries. You will be surprised by how much happened and how much you had already half forgotten.
- **Welcome the on-this-day nudge.** A reminder that a year ago today you were standing in a particular square is a small, good thing, and it tends to prompt the next trip.

## A worked example

Here is what a day in Rome looks like under this system. Six entries, a few minutes of total writing.

1. Place, 7.40am. Caffè at the bar near the hotel. Rating 4. Tag: coffee. Note: stand at the counter, half the price of sitting.
2. Place, 9.30am. Pantheon. Rating 5. Tags: landmark, architecture. Note: ticket now required, nearly empty before ten.
3. Moment, 11am. Rain started while crossing Piazza Navona, and everyone ran for the same doorway. No rating. Tag: rain. One photo of wet cobbles.
4. Place, 1pm. Pizzarium Bonci. Rating 5. Tags: pizza, food. Note: worth the queue every single time.
5. Place, 4pm. A small museum you wandered into. Rating 3. Tag: museum. Note: fine, free with the pass, would not go back.
6. Moment, 7.30pm. Sunset from the Pincio terrace, a brass band playing below. No rating. Tag: viewpoint.

That is a day you can hand to a friend as a recommendation list, reread as a story, and search in a second. It cost you less time than one long evening entry, and unlike the evening entry, it got written.
