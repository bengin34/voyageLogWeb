# Christmas markets in Europe: a 25-city checklist to tick off

There are far more good Christmas markets in Europe than anyone can see in one winter, which is the right way to think about them: not as a single trip but as a list you work through over years. Below are 25 cities, grouped by region, with what makes each market distinct and the specific thing to look for. Most markets open in the second half of November and run to 23 or 24 December, with exceptions noted. Dates shift every year, so confirm before booking.

## Germany and Austria

The heartland. German markets are the template everyone else copies: wooden stalls, a tall tree, a stage for choirs, bratwurst, and a mug of Glühwein you pay a deposit on and either return or keep. Austrian markets add the Punsch, hotter and sweeter, and the setting of imperial squares.

### 1. Nuremberg

The Christkindlesmarkt is the one people mean when they say German Christmas market. It fills the Hauptmarkt under the Frauenkirche, and it is strict about what sells: traditional goods, no plastic, no piped pop music. Look for the Nuremberg Lebkuchen, the soft gingerbread the city has baked for centuries, and the tiny Zwetschgenmännle, figures made of prunes. Eat the small Nuremberg sausages, three in a bun. Typical window: late November to 24 December.

### 2. Dresden

The Striezelmarkt claims to be Germany's oldest, and it is built around Stollen, the dense fruit bread. On a Saturday in early December the city parades a giant Stollen through town and slices it in the market. Look for the enormous stepped pyramid, the carved Erzgebirge wooden figures, and the Pflaumentoffel, chimney-sweep figures made from prunes. Typical window: late November to 24 December.

### 3. Cologne

Cologne has several markets, and the one that matters is at the foot of the cathedral, where the stalls sit under the two black spires. The others are worth a wander too: the one in the old town by the Rhine, and a harbor market along the water. Look for Reibekuchen, fried potato cakes with apple sauce, and for the view of the cathedral lit up from across the market. Typical window: late November to 23 December.

### 4. Munich

The market on Marienplatz sits under the Rathaus, with the Glockenspiel going off above it at set times. It is more urban and less picturesque than Nuremberg, and the Bavarian specialties are worth it: Schmalznudeln (fried dough), roasted almonds, and a stall or two selling nothing but nativity figures. Look for the Kripperlmarkt, a separate market devoted entirely to nativity scenes. Typical window: late November to 24 December.

### 5. Berlin

Berlin has dozens of markets, which makes it a city where you can tick off several in one day. The one at Gendarmenmarkt (currently relocated nearby during square renovations) is the elegant one with a small entry fee. Charlottenburg Palace has the prettiest setting. The one at Alexanderplatz is big and loud. Typical window: late November to late December, some running into January.

### 6. Rothenburg ob der Tauber

A small walled town that looks like a Christmas card and leans into it. The Reiterlesmarkt is modest in size and strongest in setting, with the half-timbered houses and the town walls as backdrop. Look for Schneeballen, pastry balls dusted in sugar, and for the year-round Christmas shop near the square. Typical window: late November to 23 December.

### 7. Vienna

Vienna does not have a Christmas market so much as a season of them. The big one in front of the Rathaus is the postcard, with an ice-skating path through the park. The one in front of Schönbrunn Palace is calmer and more handsome. The small one at Spittelberg, in narrow Biedermeier lanes, is the one Viennese people actually like. Look for Punsch in a dozen flavors, Kaiserschmarrn, and the way the city's own cafés become part of the circuit. Typical window: mid November to 26 December, with some markets open to New Year.

### 8. Salzburg

The Christkindlmarkt sits on Domplatz and Residenzplatz under the cathedral and the fortress, and the Alps are visible at the end of the streets. It is one of the oldest and it feels it. Look for the Krampus runs in early December, when men in terrifying horned costumes parade through, and for the choir singing from the cathedral steps on certain evenings. Typical window: late November to 26 December.

### 9. Innsbruck

Innsbruck's old-town market sits under the Golden Roof with the Nordkette mountains directly above, so close they look painted on. There is a second market on Marktplatz and a smaller one on the Bergisel hill. Look for the giant figures from Tyrolean fairy tales along the lanes, and for Kiachl, fried dough served sweet or with sauerkraut. Typical window: mid November to early January.

## Central Europe

Prague, Budapest, Kraków and the Baltic capitals have markets that are younger as tourist destinations but set in squares that no German city can match.

### 10. Prague

The market on Old Town Square is the one you have seen in photos: a tall tree, the Týn church spires behind, and the astronomical clock a few steps away. Wenceslas Square has a second market a short walk off. Look for trdelník, the spiral pastry roasted over coals, for Czech mead, and for the square at eight in the morning with light snow and nobody there. Typical window: late November to early January.

### 11. Budapest

Budapest has two main markets. The one on Vörösmarty Square is the established one, with juried craft stalls and a food court. The one in front of St. Stephen's Basilica is the atmospheric one, with light shows projected onto the basilica's facade and a small ice rink. Look for kürtőskalács (the chimney cake), lángos, and the Hungarian mulled wine, which is often better than the German sort. Typical window: mid November to early January.

### 12. Kraków

The market fills the enormous Main Square, the largest medieval square in Europe, with the Cloth Hall in the middle and St. Mary's trumpet call on the hour. Look for oscypek, smoked sheep's cheese grilled and served with cranberry jam, and for the nativity-scene competition in early December, when extraordinarily elaborate szopki are displayed near the Adam Mickiewicz monument. Typical window: late November to late December or early January.

### 13. Tallinn

A compact market on Town Hall Square, which claims the first public Christmas tree in Europe and has the medieval walls and towers right behind. It is small, cold and very pretty. Look for black pudding with sauerkraut, the Estonian Christmas staple, and for the glögi, the Nordic-style mulled wine with almonds and raisins in the cup. Typical window: late November to early January.

### 14. Zagreb

Zagreb's Advent spread across the whole center and won fans by being more fun than solemn: ice skating in King Tomislav Square, live music, a tunnel under the city turned into an installation, and the upper town lit up. Look for fritule, small doughnuts, and for the view from the Strossmayer promenade. Typical window: late November to early January.

### 15. Bratislava

A short train or boat from Vienna and much less visited. The market fills the Main Square and Hviezdoslav Square in the old town, with a strong food emphasis. Look for lokše, potato flatbreads with goose fat or poppy seeds, and for cigánska, a pork cutlet in a bun that is the local Christmas-market staple. Typical window: late November to 23 December.

## The Nordics

Nordic markets are smaller, darker and shorter, with daylight ending mid-afternoon and candles everywhere. Some run only on weekends.

### 16. Copenhagen

Tivoli Gardens turns into a Christmas market with the rides running, and the whole park is lit. It has an entry fee. The market in Nyhavn along the harbor and the one at Kongens Nytorv are free. Look for æbleskiver, round pancake puffs with jam and sugar, and for gløgg. Typical window: mid November to end of December, Tivoli often into early January.

### 17. Stockholm

The market in Stortorget, the main square of Gamla Stan, is small and old, with red stalls against the painted houses. Skansen, the open-air museum, has a larger market on weekends in December with traditional crafts and folk dancing. Look for saffron buns, pepparkakor, and the Lucia processions around 13 December. Typical window: late November to 23 December, Skansen on weekends.

### 18. Gothenburg

Liseberg amusement park hosts one of Scandinavia's largest Christmas markets, with millions of lights and a market through the park. Look for the ice-skating rink, the smoked and cured fish stalls, and the Christmas table buffets served around the city. Typical window: mid November to end of December, often closed a few days around Christmas itself.

## Western Europe

### 19. Strasbourg

Strasbourg calls itself the capital of Christmas and has a reasonable claim. The Christkindelsmärik dates to the sixteenth century, and markets spread across several squares under the cathedral and through the Petite France quarter. Look for bredele, the small Alsatian Christmas biscuits in dozens of shapes, for vin chaud, and for the great tree on Place Kléber. Typical window: late November to 24 December.

### 20. Colmar

An hour from Strasbourg and even more picture-perfect, with several small markets in the half-timbered old town and along the canals of Little Venice. It is small enough to see in an afternoon and crowded on weekends. Look for the children's market in the Petite Venise, and for the covered market with Alsatian food. Typical window: late November to late December.

### 21. Brussels

Winter Wonders runs from the Grand Place through to the Bourse and Place Sainte-Catherine, with a Ferris wheel, an ice rink and a light show on the Grand Place at set times each evening. Look for the light show specifically, and for the stalls of speculoos biscuits and Belgian waffles. Typical window: late November to early January.

### 22. Basel

Switzerland's largest Christmas market splits between Barfüsserplatz and Münsterplatz in the old town. It is handsome and orderly, with a fairy-tale forest for children on Münsterplatz. Look for Basler Läckerli, the city's spiced honey biscuit, and for raclette scraped onto bread at the stalls. Typical window: late November to 23 December.

### 23. Edinburgh

East Princes Street Gardens fill with a market, a big wheel and fairground rides below the castle. It is more fairground than craft market, but the setting under the castle at four in the afternoon is unmatched. Look for the view from the wheel, and for mulled cider rather than wine. Typical window: mid November to early January.

## Elsewhere

### 24. Montbéliard, France

Included because it is the kind of market regulars talk about when the famous ones get too crowded. A small town in eastern France near the Swiss border with a market that invites a different guest country each year and lights the whole center. Look for the Lumières de Noël and for the regional food, including the Montbéliard sausage. Typical window: late November to 24 December.

### 25. Valkenburg, Netherlands

Valkenburg's markets are underground, in the old marl caves beneath the town, with stalls lining passages cut into the rock. There are two, the Municipal Cave and the Velvet Cave, and the town above has a lit walk. Look for the cave murals and the sculptures, and for the Dutch oliebollen, deep-fried dough balls sold everywhere. These markets usually open earlier than most, in mid November, and run into late December.

## How to use this as a checklist

Twenty-five cities is a decade of winters for most people, and that is the point. Treat it as a list to tick off rather than a plan to complete.

- **Group by trip.** Nuremberg, Rothenburg and Munich are one train trip. Vienna, Bratislava and Budapest are another. Strasbourg and Colmar are a weekend. Copenhagen and Gothenburg connect by train in a few hours.
- **One new region each year.** It keeps the markets from blurring into each other.
- **Go midweek where you can.** Saturdays in Colmar and Rothenburg are crushes. Tuesdays are not.
- **Rate each market.** A rating out of five after each visit turns a list into an opinion, and an opinion is what friends ask for.

## How to journal a market visit

A Christmas market is the perfect test of a travel journal, because the visits are short, sensory, and almost identical to each other on paper. Here is what to capture so they stay distinct.

1. **Save the market as a place the moment you arrive.** Title it with the square, not just the city: Old Town Square, Rathausplatz, Striezelmarkt. Vienna alone will give you five entries.
2. **Rate it before you leave.** Do not wait until the hotel. Standing at the edge of the square with the mug in your hand, you know whether this was a 3 or a 5.
3. **Write one line about the specific thing.** Not the atmosphere, which is the same everywhere. The oscypek with cranberry. The brass band. The queue for the Stollen. The light show on the Grand Place at seven.
4. **Photograph the mug.** Every market has its own, dated, and the collection of mug photos across years is a better souvenir than the mugs.
5. **Tag it.** One tag, christmas-market, across every visit, means that in five years one search returns all of them in order.
6. **Add the moments separately.** The snow starting in Salzburg, the Krampus run, the choir on the cathedral steps. These are moments, not places, and they are the entries you will reread.
