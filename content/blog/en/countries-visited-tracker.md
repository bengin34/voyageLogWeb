A countries visited tracker does two jobs: it keeps a running count, and it keeps you honest about what that count means. The second job is the hard one. Thirty-two countries sounds precise until someone asks whether the two hours you spent in Doha airport are one of them, or whether Sicily counts separately from Italy. So this guide starts with the counting rules, then compares the ways to keep the tally: scratch-map apps such as Been, Visited and Countries Been, a plain spreadsheet, and a travel journal where the count is a by-product of the trips you have actually written down. We make VoyageLog, one of the journals in that last group, so read that section with that in mind.

## How many countries have I visited? Decide the rules first

Before you install anything, settle three questions. Your answers decide the number far more than the app does.

### Which list are you counting against?

There is no single official list of countries, and the three most common ones give very different totals.

- **193** is the number of United Nations member states. This is the strict list. Kosovo and Taiwan are not on it.
- **195** is the figure most trackers show. It is the 193 members plus the UN's two non-member observer states, the Holy See and the State of Palestine.
- **330** is the Travelers' Century Club list, which counts territories separately from their parent countries when they are geographically removed from them. Hawaii, Alaska, the Canary Islands, Sicily, Sardinia, the Azores and Greenland each count as their own entry. The club admits anyone who has been to 100 of the 330, and the current list is dated January 2022.

Pick one. The 195 list is the sensible default because it matches what most apps display and what most people mean. Use the TCC list if you want a project rather than a number; it rewards the islands and outposts that a 195-count treats as invisible.

### What counts as having been there?

This is the layover debate, and it has no right answer, only consistent ones. The TCC rule is the most generous in common use: a port-of-call or a fuel stop counts as long as you disembark and set foot in the place. Many people find that too easy. The stricter rules, in rough order of strictness:

| Situation | Generous rule | Common rule | Strict rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airside layover, never left the terminal | Counts | Does not count | Does not count |
| Cleared immigration, walked around the city for an afternoon | Counts | Counts | Does not count |
| Cruise port, six hours ashore | Counts | Counts | Does not count |
| Train or coach transit, no stop | Counts if you set foot on a platform | Does not count | Does not count |
| Overnight stay, at least one night | Counts | Counts | Counts |
| Territory of another country (Canary Islands, Hawaii) | Separate entry | Counts toward the parent country | Counts toward the parent country |
| Country you live in | Counts | Counts | Counts |

The common rule in the middle column is what most people land on: you count a country if you passed through immigration and did something there on purpose, even for an afternoon. An overnight stay is never in doubt. A terminal is never a country.

### When did the trip happen?

A count with no dates is just a number. A count with a year next to each country is a history. If you went to Prague in 2009 and again in 2024, that is the same country but two trips, and a good tracker should know the difference. This is the point where a bare scratch map starts to feel thin, and where a journal starts to earn its keep.

## Countries visited map apps compared

Scratch-map apps do one thing well. You tap a country, it fills in, the counter goes up, and you get a shareable image of the world with your corner of it coloured. Three that are current and maintained as of this writing, all free to start with paid extras. Prices are the US App Store figures on 24 October 2026; other stores vary, and prices change.

| App | What it tracks | Dates | Account | Paid tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Visited (Arriving In High Heels) | Countries, states, regions and cities; lists, itineraries, journal notes | Yes | Accounts with passwords exist; listing does not say one is required | Pro $59.99, or $9.99 monthly, $34.99 yearly |
| Countries Been (Daniel Knoblauch) | Countries, states in 25+ countries, cities; Been, Lived, Want | No dates in the listing | Sign in with Apple, Google, Facebook, email, or continue as a guest | Remove ads $1.99 |

### Been

Been is the one most people picture: a flat world map, countries you have visited filled in, a percentage for each continent. The free version covers countries and US states and lets you add visit dates. Premium adds regions in more than 25 countries, cities and airports, more detailed comparisons with friends and a timeline you can share as a video. It is quick, and the map looks good on a story. What it does not hold is anything about the trips. A filled-in Portugal tells you that you went, not whether Lisbon or Porto was the better week.

### Visited

Visited has grown from a scratch map into something closer to a travel planner, with lists, itineraries and journal notes alongside the map. It is the only one of the three that states how it counts: by official UN sovereignty status, with a setting to decide how disputed territories are treated. That is genuinely useful if you care about the 193 versus 195 question. The trade-off is the pricing, which is the steepest of the three, and an interface that has a lot of paid extras. If you want the map and nothing else, it is more app than you need.

### Countries Been

Countries Been puts the same idea on a 3D globe and adds three states for each place: Been, Lived and Want. You can sign in to sync across devices or continue as a guest and keep everything on the phone. It is the lightest of the three and the cheapest to make ad-free. Like Been, it is a map of where, not a record of what.

### What all three have in common

They are good at the picture and weak on the past. None of them is a journal. If you tap Spain, the app knows you have been to Spain. It does not know about the four separate trips, the first one in 2011, or the tapas bar in Barcelona you rated 5 and the one you rated 2. The count is the product, and the count is all you get. For a lot of people that is exactly enough. For anyone who later asks "when was I actually in Vienna" it is not.

## The spreadsheet as a travel counter app

Before any of these apps existed, the countries-visited list was a spreadsheet, and it is still the most flexible option. One row per trip: start date, end date, country, cities, a note. In Google Sheets, `COUNTUNIQUE` on the country column gives you the number (Excel needs a different formula for the same count), a pivot table gives you the most visited country and the trips per year, and nothing is behind a paywall.

A minimal layout that works:

| Start | End | Country | Cities | Rule | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2024-12-06 | 2024-12-10 | Czechia | Prague | Overnight | Christmas markets, Charles Bridge at 7am |
| 2024-12-10 | 2024-12-13 | Austria | Vienna | Overnight | Café Central, Belvedere |
| 2025-04-18 | 2025-04-18 | Qatar | Doha | Layover | Airside only, does not count |

The Rule column is the trick. Keep the layovers in the sheet so you are not tempted to recount them later, but tag them so the formula can leave them out. The weaknesses are the obvious ones: a spreadsheet does not take photos, it is a chore to fill in on a phone standing outside a museum, and it draws no map without extra work.

### Why a count attached to entries beats a bare number

- **It answers the follow-up question.** "How many countries" is almost always followed by "which was your favourite" or "when were you last in Italy". A scratch map shrugs. A journal opens the trip.
- **It cannot be inflated by accident.** A country shows up because you saved a trip there, not because you tapped a map, so the Doha layover stays out unless you deliberately make a trip card for it.
- **It survives the second visit.** Prague in 2009 and Prague in 2024 are two trips, two sets of entries, one country. The map colours it once; the timeline keeps both.
- **It keeps counting when you stop caring about the count.** Most people lose interest in the number somewhere in the thirties. The entries stay useful, and the Year in Travel look-back still lays the year out month by month.
- **The data is yours to take.** With Pro, export to Excel and you have the spreadsheet from the previous section, generated rather than typed. A free data backup moves your trips and entries to a new phone, and the Pro ZIP export brings the photos and videos too.

If what you want is a prettier version of the Been map with no writing at all, this is the wrong tool and Been is the right one. If you were already going to write things down, the count comes free.

## Rebuilding your count from scratch

Whatever tracker you pick, the first job is the same: reconstructing where you have actually been. A workable order.

1. **Passports, old and current.** Stamps give you countries and often exact dates. Schengen stamps only ever marked entry to and exit from the zone, and since April 2026 the EU's digital Entry/Exit System has replaced stamping for non-EU visitors, so for the countries inside the zone and for recent trips you will need another source.
2. **Photo library by place.** Both Apple Photos and Google Photos can show your pictures on a map. Work through it year by year; a cluster of photos is a trip, and the first and last photo give you its dates.
3. **Email.** Search for boarding passes, hotel confirmations and rental agreements. Short trips you had forgotten tend to turn up here.
4. **Saved places in your maps app.** Starred restaurants are often the only record of a weekend.
5. **Ask whoever travelled with you.** Someone else remembers the night in Girona that you have filed under Barcelona.

As each trip surfaces, write it down with a year and a country before moving on, and decide its status under your rule immediately. Then count. It is normal to end up two or three lower than the number you had been telling people, and one or two higher than you expected, as the forgotten short trips and the inflated layovers cancel out.

## Been app alternative: picking the tool for the job

Match the tool to what you want from the number:

- **You want the map image and a counter, nothing else.** Been. Countries Been if you prefer a globe and guest mode.
- **You care about disputed territories and want control over the counting rule.** Visited, which lets you set sovereignty handling, at a higher price.
- **You want the TCC 330 list.** A spreadsheet, or an app that explicitly supports TCC territories; the three above are built around the 195 list.
