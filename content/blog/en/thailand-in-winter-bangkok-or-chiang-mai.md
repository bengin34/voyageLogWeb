# Thailand in winter: Bangkok or Chiang Mai for a city break

For Thailand in winter, the cool season, choose one city and build each day around something that works in any weather. Bangkok suits you if you want a big river city with palaces, temples and plenty of variety. Chiang Mai suits you if you prefer a smaller Old City where temples sit close together and a hill temple waits outside the centre.

"Cool season" is a label, not a forecast. Days can still be warm and humid, and the mornings and evenings are where the label is most likely to hold. This guide does not give temperatures or promise a particular feel for your dates, because they vary by year and by place.

Everything below is a proposed framework, not a tested tour or a report on current conditions. Opening hours, entry rules and festival dates change, so confirm them with each operator before you book.

## Bangkok or Chiang Mai: the pace test

Before comparing sights, decide what kind of days you want. Rank the two cities on that single question.

- **Bangkok, the large-city day.** The Chao Phraya River divides the old riverside districts from the western bank, and the big names sit around it: the Grand Palace, Wat Pho and Wat Arun. Distances between places are larger, so a day works best when it stays in one area.
- **Chiang Mai, the walkable-centre day.** The walled Old City holds temples such as Wat Phra Singh and Wat Chedi Luang within one compact area. A day can be a loose loop on foot with long pauses.

Now add a second question: how much do you want to handle logistics? Bangkok rewards a day with one clear destination and a short list of alternatives. Chiang Mai rewards a day with almost no plan at all. If you hesitate between them, choose the one where your slowest, least impressive day still sounds enjoyable. That is a better test than choosing the one with the best photograph.

With three days, one city is usually enough. With five or six, a second base becomes reasonable, as long as you accept losing part of a day to the move.

## Bangkok in the cool season: a riverside heritage day

Bangkok rewards a day built around one large heritage visit and a loose surrounding walk. Choose between the Grand Palace area and a quieter neighbourhood day, not both, unless you genuinely want a long one.

**Proposed framework for one Bangkok day.** Start early with the main heritage visit while you are fresh. If you choose the Grand Palace, check the current visiting rules and dress requirements with the operator, and allow time at the entrance. Wat Pho is a natural second stop for the same area, and Wat Arun sits across the river. Whether you cross by public ferry or another route is something to check locally on the day, not to assume from this article.

After lunch, slow down. A river-side pause, a small museum or a covered market is a better afternoon than a third major sight. Large sights absorb time, so choose the section you want and accept leaving the rest.

Two practical notes. First, temples ask for modest dress and for shoes to come off at many entrances, so carry something light to cover shoulders and knees. Second, some sites have closing days or special ceremonies that change access, so check the day you chose.

## Chiang Mai in the cool season: the Old City and a hill temple

Chiang Mai is small enough that you do not need a clever route. The usual structure is the Old City on foot and one outing beyond it.

**Proposed framework for one Chiang Mai day.** Spend the morning inside the Old City with no fixed list. Wat Phra Singh and Wat Chedi Luang are two clear reference points, and smaller temples such as Wat Phan Tao sit beside them. Take a long lunch. In the late afternoon, or on a second day, consider the hill temple of Wat Phra That Doi Suthep outside the city.

Treat that outing as a separate decision. Check how people usually reach it, what the current arrangements are and what clothing it requires, and do this on the morning itself rather than weeks earlier. If the answer is unclear or the sky looks flat, spend the day in the Old City instead. Views from higher ground depend on visibility, and a hazy view is a smaller reward than the walk.

The Old City is also easy to reduce. If you only have one day, it fits one decision: a loop of temples on foot, with a long pause in the middle.

## A city-only fallback plan for a hot or wet day

Here is a three-day framework you can adapt to either city. It assumes no outdoor reservations and no ambition to see every highlight.

- **Day one, arrival and one anchor.** Arrive, settle in and choose a single anchor with a defined end point, such as a single temple complex or a walk along one stretch of the river or Old City moat. Stop early.
- **Day two, the main visit.** Choose the larger sight. In Bangkok that might be the Grand Palace area; in Chiang Mai, the Old City temples. Put a long lunch in the middle of the day, ideally somewhere shaded or air-conditioned, and keep the evening short.
- **Day three, a different shape.** Swap outdoor for indoor, or the reverse, depending on how you feel. A neighbourhood walk, a small museum, a market or a second visit to your favourite pause all count.

Every day has one fixed anchor and one optional extra. If the extra is not possible, you have still had a day. The mechanism is deliberately modest.

If you want more, add the hill temple outside Chiang Mai or a riverside crossing in Bangkok as a separate day, doable only when you have checked the conditions. Do not move your fixed anchors to make room for it.

## Choosing your hours: heat, crowds and rest

In a warm climate, the most useful planning decision is not which sight but which hour. Put the exposed, walking-heavy parts of the day in the early morning or late afternoon and use the middle for lunch and rest. This is a general habit, not a prediction, and it works whether the day turns out hot or mild.

Do not assume the season makes anything quiet or cheap. Popular sights can be busy on any day, and the cool season is a popular time to travel. If a crowd would ruin a visit for you, check opening hours and choose the earliest slot rather than guessing which day is empty.

Also keep one evening with nothing booked. A late start, a long lunch or a slow river crossing should not cost you a reservation.

## Before you leave: a departure checklist

Work through this list a few days ahead, then again briefly on the morning of each outdoor visit.

- Entry requirements for your nationality, checked with the official authorities. This guide gives no visa advice.
- Opening days and any ticket or dress rules for each anchor sight.
- Whether your indoor alternative is open on the same day as the outdoor plan.
- Accommodation access: stairs, narrow lanes and how you will carry luggage.
- Shoes that are easy to take off, and a light layer that covers shoulders and knees for temples.
- Sun cover, water and a plan for where you will pause in the hottest hours.
- Local transport information for any move between cities, checked close to travel.
- Offline copies of tickets and addresses, saved separately from any notes app.

## What this framework cannot tell you

It cannot tell you what the weather will do, which sights will be busy on your dates or whether a particular outing is open. It does not replace current information from the operators and tourism offices responsible for each place. It also leaves out islands, beaches and trekking, which are different trips with their own planning.

It deliberately keeps the number of places low. If you prefer a faster pace, you may fit more, but test that choice against a hot, tiring day first.
