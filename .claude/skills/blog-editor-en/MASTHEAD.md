# Masthead lens: English desk

The quality bar for the English desk, taken from publications that English-language travellers already trust. Read it with section 10 of `.claude/skills/blog-editor-core/REFERENCE.md`: we borrow the standard, never the content. Use it as a separate pass after the line edit (see `SKILL.md`).

Researched on 24 October 2026. Every summary below is a paraphrase in our own words. **Stated** means the publication says it on a policy or about page (URL given, so the owner can re-check). **Inferred** means we read it off their published articles; it is a pattern, not a promise they make.

## The benchmarks

### 1. Lonely Planet (destination service journalism)

**Why it is the benchmark.** Lonely Planet is the guidebook name most English-speaking travellers recognize, and its articles rank for the same practical queries our seasonal and how-to pieces target. Its whole brand rests on information a reader can act on, which is the standard our service sections must meet.

**Editorial QC, stated:**
- Original editorial content and recommendations come from staff, editors and local contributors using their own reporting and judgement. One named editor approves every original piece before it goes out. (https://www.lonelyplanet.com/legal/ai-policy, also served at https://www.lonelyplanet.com/ai-policy)
- AI is limited to support work: spotting patterns in search trends or public reviews, brainstorming angles, suggesting headlines, tagging. An editor checks any output before readers see it. AI is never used to produce reporting, reviews or recommendations, to invent quotes or traveller experiences, or to fake local knowledge. Reader-facing AI summaries only condense the human-written article below them and are labelled. (same page)
- The policy admits that ground facts decay (places close, prices move) and asks readers to report what needs updating. (same page)
- Guidebook writers may not take free or discounted services in exchange for coverage; guidebook recommendations may not be based on press trips at all. Web pitches based on a press trip must say so up front. Contributors are chosen for local expertise, not for access to a press office. (https://www.lonelyplanet.com/about)

**Inferred from published work** (a German Christmas markets list, dated 13 October 2026):
- A byline with a short bio that establishes why this writer knows the place; the publish date sits under the headline.
- The affiliate disclosure is the first thing above the body, in one plain sentence.
- One general seasonal caveat early (typical opening period, exact dates and hours vary, check ahead), then concrete dates for the current season only where the writer has them, with the year.
- Distances given as a figure from a city the reader knows, in km with miles.
- Honest friction inside the recommendation: a famous pick is admitted to be crowded, with a timing tip to avoid the worst of it.
- Each pick carries one actionable tip, not a summary.

### 2. Afar (narrative and service travel magazine)

**Why it is the benchmark.** Afar is the US magazine that most consistently combines reported travel writing with service pieces, and it is explicit about independence and hosted travel. It shows what "quietly confident, specific, unhurried" looks like at magazine level, and it is the closest public stand-in for the Condé Nast Traveller register the English skill names (see "Not used" below).

**Editorial QC, stated:**
- Independently owned, and follows the editorial integrity standards of the American Society of Magazine Editors. Affiliate commissions are disclosed; recommended products are vetted by editors and driven by editorial standards rather than advertising. Advertiser content is labelled as paid. (https://www.afar.com/about/editorial-standards-and-team)
- Contributors must say in the pitch whether they accepted, or plan to accept, a press trip or any comped support. News pieces bring in analysts, executives or associations to give context, and are expected to cover the pain points of a change as well as the upside. Service ("intel") pitches must show how the piece helps a reader travel more easily, and why this writer is the right person to write it. (https://www.afar.com/about/pitch-guidelines)

**Inferred from published work:** we could not study enough Afar articles in this session to state patterns; treat Afar here as a policy benchmark only.

### 3. Which? (product-review benchmark for our app comparisons)

**Why it is the benchmark.** Our comparison and alternatives articles are product reviews in all but name. Which? is the UK's independent consumer tester and has the most complete public statement of how a review earns trust: who pays, how things are selected, how verdicts are reached, what happens when a review goes stale. It replaces Wirecutter, which we could not access (see "Not used" below).

**Editorial QC, stated:**
- Funded by members, no third-party display ads, no payment for reviews. It buys nearly everything it tests and accepts no free products, so a company cannot withhold access or lean on a score. Companies get no approval over content before publication, and how cooperative a company is does not affect the verdict. Staff take no freebies, paid trips or lavish hospitality. Affiliate commissions exist, are disclosed, and are generated outside editorial control. AI may help with angles or structuring research, but it does not write findings; testing and advice stay human-led. Errors are corrected quickly. (https://www.which.co.uk/about-which/our-statement-of-editorial-independence-aFnFu0X8HMSl, dated 25 August 2026)
- Every product in a category goes through the same tests, so verdicts are comparable. It publishes negative verdicts ("Don't Buy", security notices) as well as recommendations, and a safety failure overrides good performance elsewhere. (https://www.which.co.uk/about-which/which-tests-aO40c9I5iXNM)
- Coverage is chosen deliberately (what many people use and ask about) and reviewed regularly. When a review goes out of date it is removed rather than left to mislead. (https://www.which.co.uk/about-which/why-we-test-what-we-test-amoEV2R0joh0)
- An external advisory panel checks that methods are sound. (https://www.which.co.uk/about-which/research-methods-a3i5n6T4Ucnz)

**Inferred from published work** (a lip balm round-up, dated 30 March 2026):
- The standfirst names which products were included, new and familiar, before any verdict.
- A short method block near the top: how many items, the criteria by name, what each criterion means for the reader.
- Contradictions in the results are admitted rather than smoothed over.
- Two separate dates under the results table: when it was tested and when the page was last checked, plus a caveat that prices vary by retailer.

## Editor's lens: questions for every English article

Ask each one after the line edit. A "no" is a fix, a cut or a line in the report; never a citation or link in the article (house rules win, REFERENCE.md section 10).

1. **Could a named editor sign this off?** Would you put your own name under every factual sentence? If one sentence makes you hesitate, it is unverified: check it or generalize it (REFERENCE.md section 5).
2. **Does every recommendation come from something we actually did?** We checked apps' own sites, help pages and store listings, and we know VoyageLog from `en.js`. We did not test competitors on trips, visit the markets or ride the trains. Any sentence that implies hands-on experience we do not have ("in our testing", "we found the battery drain") goes.
3. **Is the selection explained?** A comparison should say in a sentence or two why these apps and not others (what readers in this market search for and install), and the same criteria should be applied to every app in it, VoyageLog included.
4. **Same questions for every app?** If the table or sections cover account, offline use, tracking, export and price for one app, they cover them for all, or the gap is stated ("Journi does not say whether ...") rather than silently skipped.
5. **Are downsides stated as plainly as strengths?** Every app gets at least one concrete limit, VoyageLog first among them (no route tracking, no sync, no web or iPad, free tier limited). The "when VoyageLog is the wrong choice" section names who should install something else, and what.
6. **Would a sceptical competitor's product manager accept every sentence about their app?** A claim about another company's product must be true today on their own pages. Their marketing adjectives do not carry over into our sentences.
7. **Is the disclosure plain, early and once?** One sentence in the first two paragraphs saying we make VoyageLog. Not buried, not repeated as false modesty in every section.
8. **Is anything time-sensitive anchored?** Prices, free-tier limits, feature availability, event dates and opening seasons either carry the year or month they refer to ("the 2026 season"), or are phrased so they stay true ("usually opens in late November"). Never "currently", "this year" or "recently" without an anchor.
9. **Does the `updated` date tell the truth?** If you set `updated` to today, every time-sensitive claim in the piece has been re-checked today, not only the paragraph you edited.
10. **Would a reader acting on a practical detail be let down?** Opening times, queue rules, a café that still exists, a market's last day: verified on the official source, or written as a general pattern with "check the city's own listing before you go" in prose (no link).
11. **Is friction honest?** Crowds, costs, weather, closures, the catch in a free tier. Service writing that only praises reads like an advert.
12. **Is each list item earning its place?** Every entry in a list or ranking needs a reason specific to it, and the reason should be one a reader could check. If two entries share a justification, merge or cut one. Do not rank where the order means nothing; say what each is best at instead.
13. **Does each section end with something to do?** A step, a setting, a timing, a choice. Cut endings that summarize.
14. **Are numbers and places given the way a global reader can use them?** Distances from a city they would know, both km and miles where it helps; currency only when verified for a named storefront; no assumption that the reader is in the US or UK.
15. **Is anything presented as reporting that is really inference?** "Users report", "many travellers say", "people often" need a basis. If we cannot point to one internally, rephrase as general advice in our own voice.
16. **Does any sentence simulate experience or local expertise we do not have?** No invented anecdotes, no "a reader told us", no composite traveller presented as real. Illustrative examples are fine when they read as examples ("a note like 'the 8am slot was empty'").
17. **Does the voice stay plural and accountable?** "We" throughout, including the FAQ; no named author, no implied individual tester.
18. **If this page were six months old, what would be wrong first?** Name it in the report under "Still uncertain, for the owner" so the next refresh starts there.

## What we never borrow

- Their text, headlines, standfirsts, FAQ questions or recognisable phrasing, even rewritten closely.
- Their list selections and rankings: which markets, apps or places they chose, and in what order. Our picks come from our own checks and our own market research.
- Their signature formats: per-entry "best for" labels, branded tip boxes, named award or ranking franchises, rating symbols and verdict badges, "how we test" blocks styled like theirs.
- Their testing claims: they test on the ground or in labs; we do not, and we never imply it.
- Their sourcing as our sourcing: nothing from their articles becomes a fact in ours. A fact they print is a lead to verify on the primary source, never the source.
- Their names: never mention, quote, compare ourselves to or link these publications in an article, the FAQ or the frontmatter.
