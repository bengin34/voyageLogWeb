# Masthead lens: Spanish desk (es)

## The benchmarks

### 1. El Viajero (El País), Spain

**Why it is the benchmark.** The travel section of Spain's paper of record, published under the best-known newsroom style book in the Spanish language. Spanish readers on both sides of the Atlantic treat its register as the reference for clean, precise, checked prose. `SKILL.md` already names it as the standard.

**Editorial QC, in our words** [Policy, not re-read]:
- El País publishes its *Libro de estilo* openly, in print and online. It is both a language guide and an ethics code: facts checked before publication, sources identified, information kept apart from opinion and from advertising, precise rules for numbers, names and toponyms.
- Errors are rectified in print, and a readers' ombudsman (*Defensora del lector*) answers complaints publicly.
- A newsroom charter (*Estatuto de Redacción*) protects editorial independence from the owners and from commercial pressure.
- The style book also addresses conflicts of interest. We could not re-read the exact rule on gifts and invited press trips; do not cite any detail of it.
- AI-content policy: not verified this session.

**What its service pieces do** [Inferred, from general familiarity with published work, not re-checked this session]: practical data kept apart from the narrative, named places with specific detail instead of adjectives, dated pieces.

**URLs to re-check:** https://www.estilo.elpais.com/ (style book), https://elpais.com/elviajero/ (section), https://elpais.com/info/estatuto-de-redaccion/ (tried; address not confirmed). The *Defensora del lector* section lives on elpais.com; its exact address was not confirmed.

### 2. Lonely Planet en español (geoPlaneta), Spain and Latin America

**Why it is the benchmark.** The most widely used Spanish-language guidebook brand in both Spain and Latin America, published in Spanish by geoPlaneta (Barcelona). It is what many of our readers check before a trip, and like us it signs most web pieces with a corporate byline, not a named author.

**Editorial QC, in our words** [Policy, read]:
- Authors travel to research and keep revising their reviews; they may visit places without announcing themselves to stay objective. The publisher aims to renew each guide about every two years, with stated exceptions.
- Authors accept no payment, gifts or free services. The printed guides carry no advertising, and businesses cannot pay to be listed.
- The website does carry advertising, and says advertisers get no preferential treatment and that no payment or favour buys an opinion or a recommendation.
- Selection is openly limited by space: being left out is explicitly not a negative verdict.
- Readers and businesses can send corrections; they are passed to the authors, who decide.
- AI-content policy: none found on the pages read.

**What its service pieces do** [Inferred, from two pages read on 2026-10-24]:
- Good practice: each neighbourhood is framed by its character, the best time to go, how walkable it is and who it suits. Caveats sit inside the prose (an area with few sights, a gap in metro coverage), and advice comes with its reason (why cycling in central Rome is a bad idea). Sponsored posts are labelled as sponsored in the category listing.
- Lesson by counter-example: a Rome transport guide from 2022 states exact fares and metro hours with no update date and no caveat, and a destination hub still shows pandemic-era advice without a warning. The detail outlived its currency. Our `updated` field and durable phrasing exist to prevent exactly that.

**URLs:** https://www.lonelyplanet.es/faq, https://www.lonelyplanet.es/quienes-somos, https://www.lonelyplanet.es/europa/espana/madrid, https://www.lonelyplanet.es/blog/consejos-para-viajar/como-moverse-por-roma

### 4. Xataka (Webedia), product reviews and app guides

**Why it is the benchmark.** Spain's largest consumer-tech outlet, also widely read in Latin America. It publishes how it reviews and how it deals with companies, and its buying guides are the format closest to our app comparisons.

**Editorial QC, in our words** [Policy, read]:
- Reviews are not for sale and products are not kept in exchange for coverage. Editors decide what to review.
- Each review says at the end where the test unit came from (brand loan, bought, an editor's own device).
- Brands have no control over a review and do not see it before publication. Embargoes are accepted only without extra conditions, and incomplete coverage is not rushed out to meet one.
- Formats are named honestly: a full review means several days of normal and heavy use; first impressions cover only design and handling, with no verdict on performance or battery; comparisons test devices under the same conditions; buying guides are refreshed as products arrive.
- Scores exist only in full reviews, are the average of visible sub-scores, are checked by a second specialist, leave price out, and belong to the date of the review.
- Affiliate commissions are disclosed. The newsroom is separate from the e-commerce and branded-content teams, and a contributor with a tie to a brand may not write about it.
- Small merchandise is fine; gifts above 250 euros go back. Press trips are accepted only when newsworthy and with no obligation to cover.
- AI-content policy: none found on the pages read.

**What its buying guides do** [Inferred, one guide read on 2026-10-24]: a "who it suits" line and a main weakness for every product, which is the right instinct. The same guide also shows what to avoid: selection criteria placed after the picks, two different prices for the same product in table and body, no update note, and no statement of whether the products were tested or chosen from specifications.

**URLs:** https://www.xataka.com/como-hacemos-reviews-xataka, https://www.xataka.com/relaciones-empresas, https://www.xataka.com/quienes-somos, https://www.xataka.com/seleccion/mejores-proyectores-cine-casa

### Secondary reference: OCU (consumer organisation, Spain)

**Why.** For Spanish readers OCU's comparisons define what "objective" means in a product comparison. We cannot test like a lab; we can be as explicit about method.

**Editorial QC, in our words** [Policy, read]: its own team picks products for representativeness (best sellers, new releases, comparable ranges); products are bought anonymously, the way any consumer would; independent labs run tests to OCU's protocols; the criteria are named (quality, ease of use, safety, price); its award labels are defined publicly; funding comes mainly from members, with no economic aid or commercial messages from companies. It discloses that products outside the lab programme are rated with AI tools working from objective characteristics and technical results. The full ethics code is a PDF we did not read.

**URLs:** https://www.ocu.org/info/quienes-somos, https://www.ocu.org/info/quienes-somos/nuestros-analisis, https://www.ocu.org/info/quienes-somos/transparencia, https://www.ocu.org/info/codigo-etico-ocu

## What the best of them consistently do

1. **Say how the selection was made, before the selection.** Method first, picks second (Xataka's policy, OCU; Xataka's own guide shows the cost of reversing it).
2. **Separate what was tested, what the maker says and what is judgment.** Format names carry promises: a first impression never pretends to be a review.
3. **Give every pick a reader it suits and, by implication, one it does not.**
4. **Date volatile facts or make them durable.** Exact fares without a date are a liability within two years.
5. **One figure per fact.** The same number in table, body and summary.
6. **Disclose commercial interest once, plainly, where the reader sees it.**
7. **Admit the list is a selection.** Leaving something out is not a verdict.
8. **Correct visibly and quickly.** A correction route exists and errors are fixed, not defended.
9. **Give the reason with the advice.**

## Editor's lens: questions for every Spanish article

Ask each one of the finished line edit. A "no" means fix it in the article, or cut the claim.

1. **Method in one sentence.** In a comparison or alternatives piece, does the text say how the apps were chosen and what we actually did (checked each app's own site and its Spanish or Mexican store page), and never more than that?
2. **Criteria before picks.** Are the criteria (account, privacy, offline, export, price model) named before the list, and is every app judged on the same ones?
3. **Same yardstick for VoyageLog.** Is VoyageLog held to those criteria with its weaknesses in the same plain words used for rivals?
4. **Disclosure.** Is «hacemos VoyageLog» stated once, plainly, in the first two paragraphs, and not repeated as a sales line?
5. **Traceable facts.** Can every competitor, price, date and place claim be traced in the report to a primary source checked today, with vendor promises framed as the vendor's own?
6. **No borrowed experience.** Is there any sentence implying hands-on testing, a trip we took or an individual expert («en nuestras pruebas», «cuando lo usamos en Oaxaca», «como viajera experta»)? Rewrite to what we did or cut.
7. **Fact, maker's claim, opinion.** Can the reader tell which sentences are verified facts, which repeat what an app says about itself, and which are our judgment?
8. **One figure per fact.** Does each number, limit or feature read identically in table, body, FAQ and description?
9. **Ageing.** Is every volatile fact (price, free-tier limit, season, event date, entry rule) either verified today and phrased to age well, or made durable («gratuita, con un plan de pago»)? Is `updated` set when a fact changed?
10. **Who it suits, who should skip.** Does every option, VoyageLog included, have a reader it fits and one it does not, with an honest «cuándo VoyageLog no es la opción» where the brief asks for it?
11. **Limits stated flatly.** Are VoyageLog's non-features (no route tracking, no cloud sync, no web version) said plainly where they matter, not softened or left to the FAQ?
12. **Selection, not universe.** Does the article avoid «la mejor app», «todas las apps» and any ranking it cannot justify?
13. **Both shores.** Is everything recommended available and relevant in Spain and in the main Latin American markets, with no EU-only fact (roaming, ETIAS, euro prices) presented as universal?
14. **A reason with every instruction.** Does each piece of advice carry its why (export before changing phone because nothing lives on a server)?
15. **Checked, specific places.** Are cities, venues, events and seasons named precisely and verified on the official site, and is no detail kept only because it sounds good?
16. ***Libro de estilo* precision.** Established Spanish toponyms (Lisboa, Múnich, Nueva York), numbers and units per house style, nouns and verbs carrying the facts instead of adjectives?
17. **Service before promotion.** Outside the `{{cta}}`, does any paragraph read like advertising copy rather than help?
18. **Invisible sourcing, complete trace.** No links, no «según», no citations in the article, yet a source in the report for every claim kept?
19. **Correction-ready.** Does each volatile fact live in one place so a fix is one sentence, and does the report flag the claims most likely to go stale?
20. **The desk test.** Would an editor at a serious Spanish travel section cut any sentence as filler, unchecked or promotional? Then cut it.

Record lens fixes under "What changed" in the report like any other edit.

## What we never borrow

- Their text: headlines, ledes, standfirsts, captions, FAQ wording, not even as a close paraphrase.
- Their selections: rankings, top lists, the order of a list, their choice of apps, places or products.
- Their signature formats and labels: award names, annual ranking brands, named sections, table column headings, scoring scales.
- Recognisable phrasing, slogans or a section's characteristic tone markers.
- Their findings presented as ours: measurements, test results, scores, prices they quoted.
- Their practices we cannot honestly follow: lab or field testing, unannounced visits, numeric scores, a named expert byline. Our byline stays «nosotros».
- Any mention of them in an article: no name, quote, link or «como hacen las grandes revistas».
