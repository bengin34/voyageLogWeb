## 0. The shared floor: Pressekodex (Deutscher Presserat)

**Why it matters here.** Nearly every German publisher has signed the Presserat's voluntary commitment, so this code is the baseline behind ZEIT, SZ, GEO and Merian. A German reader who distrusts an app comparison is, without knowing it, measuring it against these rules.

**What it asks, in our words** (version of 19 March 2025):
- **Ziffer 2, Sorgfalt:** check before you publish, report faithfully, mark assumptions and unconfirmed claims as such. Headlines and captions must not distort what the text says. Symbolic or staged images must be labelled.
- **Ziffer 3, Richtigstellung:** a published error is corrected promptly and on the outlet's own initiative; online, the correction is tied to the original piece and marked as a correction.
- **Ziffer 7, Trennung von Werbung und Redaktion:** commercial interests must not steer editorial content, and material serving the publisher's own interest must be recognisable as such. Richtlinie 7.2 warns that coverage of a product slides into Schleichwerbung when it goes beyond what readers' interest justifies, and asks for special care with PR material.
- **Ziffer 15, Vergünstigungen:** no gifts or invitations that could look like influence; when reporting draws on an invited trip, the funding has to be made visible.
- **AI:** there is no separate AI section, but the preamble extends editorial responsibility to all content regardless of how it was produced, explicitly including artificially generated content.

**What this means for VoyageLog.** We are a manufacturer writing about our own category, which is exactly the situation Ziffer 7 is about. Our disclosure sentence is not a courtesy; it is what makes the article editorial rather than advertising. The AI clause means every sentence is ours to answer for, however it was drafted.

Source: https://www.presserat.de/pressekodex.html (commitment overview: https://www.presserat.de/selbstverpflichtung.html)

## 1. Stiftung Warentest: the benchmark for comparisons

**Why it is the benchmark.** In DACH, "Test" and "Testsieger" mean Stiftung Warentest to most readers. Its comparisons set the expectation of what a fair product verdict looks like: fixed criteria, the same procedure for every product, no money from the tested companies. Our app comparisons are read against that expectation even though we are the opposite of neutral.

**Editorial QC approach, in our words** (stated policy):
- **Independence by structure:** a foundation with no advertising in its publications and no affiliate income; it lives mainly from selling its results. Its own summary of the principles: anonymous purchase, testing at independent institutes, judgment only on objective results, ad-free publication.
- **Selection is theirs alone:** topic ideas come from reader questions, search queries on its site, consumer advice centres and market observation; manufacturers cannot commission a test or choose which products go in.
- **Method before results:** a test design lists every test point first. An advisory panel (consumer advocates, independent experts and provider representatives) reviews whether the criteria are complete, but the foundation alone decides the final criteria. Published tests explain how they tested and how much each criterion counts.
- **Real-world samples:** products are bought anonymously the way a consumer would buy them, so nobody gets a specially prepared sample.
- **Checked before release:** a separate verification team checks every detail of the results before publication.
- **Corrections in the open:** it states that it makes mistakes and that its priority is immediate transparency and correction; its FAQ gives a past case where it republished corrected results and recalled an issue. Providers who dispute results can ask for a review.
- No AI policy was found on the pages read.

**What we take from it.** State the criteria before the list. Apply the same criteria to every app, including ours. Describe our method truthfully, which is a desk check of vendors' own pages and the German store listing, never a test. Correct openly when we were wrong.

## 2. ZEIT Reisen and SZ Reise: the benchmark for travel writing

**Why they are the benchmark.** The travel sections of DIE ZEIT and the Süddeutsche Zeitung are what educated DACH readers mean by a good Reiseteil: written by named authors, sachlich, with practical service kept apart from the reportage. `SKILL.md` already names them as the tone to edit towards.

**Status: inferred.** Neither site could be fetched from this environment, so no policy page was read this session. Everything below is **inferred from their published work and general knowledge of the trade**, not from a stated policy. Owner: check whether each house publishes editorial guidelines, an AI statement and a corrections practice, and replace this note with what you find.
- Section pages to start from (not fetched): https://www.zeit.de/entdecken/reisen and https://www.sueddeutsche.de/reise
- Both publishers are understood to work under the Pressekodex (section 0).

**Practices seen in published work (inferred):**
- **Trip funding disclosed:** pieces based on a supported research trip carry a short line naming who supported it, usually at the end. This is the Ziffer 15 rule in practice.
- **Service apart from story:** practical information (getting there, where to stay, where to get information) sits in a separate block after the text, so the narrative never turns into a brochure and the reader can find the facts without rereading.
- **Prices and seasons with context:** practical figures come with a currency and a time reference, and they are kept few, because they age fastest.
- **Downsides named:** good reportage in these sections says when a place is crowded, overpriced or past its best. The criticism is what makes the recommendation credible.
- **The author was there:** their authority comes from on-the-ground reporting under a personal byline. This is the one practice we must **not** imitate (see the lens, questions 9 and 17).

**What we take from it.** Disclosure as a plain sentence, practical facts that the reader can find and trust, honest downsides, a sober register. Not their reporting authority: we did not travel for these articles and never write as if we had.

## 3. Merian: curation and credits, and a warning

**Why it is a benchmark.** Merian is the long-running German premium travel magazine (in print for more than 75 years, published by Jahreszeiten Verlag, Hamburg), relaunched in 2023. It positions itself on careful curation and thorough research.

**What is stated** (self-description page): a carefully curated selection of travel themes; reportages, essays and interviews described as thoroughly researched; destination tips and service pieces alongside. No public page on independence, press trips, AI or corrections was found.

**What its web service pieces consistently do (inferred from two list articles, October 2026):**
- Byline and publication date on the piece; every image credited, with press-supplied photos labelled as press material.
- Fixed list format with a stated number of entries, each entry with a name, location and a short characterisation.
- **But:** the selection criteria are implied, not stated; prices, seasons and booking facts are rare; downsides are almost absent; no update note. The tone of these lists is closer to promotion than to service.

**What we take from it.** The good habits: a visible date, honest image credits, entries with a clear identity. The warning: a curated list without stated criteria and without a single limit reads like advertising. Our lists must state why each item is there and what speaks against it.

Sources: https://www.merian.de/merian-reisemagazin (self-description), https://www.merian.de/impressum (publisher). Service pieces analysed for structure only: https://www.merian.de/europa/spanien/kanarische-inseln-hotel-tipps and https://www.merian.de/fernreisen/die-besten-lodges

## Editor's lens: questions for every German article

Ask these after the line edit. A "no" means a fix in the article or a line in the report.

1. **Disclosure (Ziffer 7):** If VoyageLog is compared or recommended, is „VoyageLog machen wir selbst“ (or an equally plain sentence) in the first or second paragraph, once, unhedged?
2. **Reader interest first (Richtlinie 7.2):** Would a reader who never installs VoyageLog still get a complete answer to the query? Does VoyageLog take more space than the question justifies?
3. **Criteria before the list:** Does a comparison say, before the first entry, what was judged (offline use, account requirement, export, price model, platforms) and why these apps are in it and others are not?
4. **Same criteria for everyone:** Is VoyageLog judged on the same criteria as the others, including the ones where it loses (automatic route tracking, cloud sync, web or iPad)?
5. **Dealbreakers in the verdict:** Does a decisive weakness of any app, ours included, appear in the recommendation itself rather than in a footnote-like sentence further down?
6. **One honest limit per entry:** Does every recommended app, place, market or method have at least one stated downside or „nicht für dich, wenn …“? Does the piece keep its „Wann VoyageLog die falsche Wahl ist“ section where it fits?
7. **Truthful method:** Does the article describe only what was actually done (vendor pages and the German App Store or Google Play listing checked)? No „Test“, „getestet“, „Testsieger“, „Note“ or „im Test“: those promise a Warentest-style lab test.
8. **Every claim checked today (Ziffer 2):** Is every competitor, price, date, season and place fact verified on a primary source today and recorded with its URL in the report, since the article itself carries no sources?
9. **No on-the-ground claims:** Does any sentence imply we were there, tasted, queued or counted (crowds, waiting times, atmosphere on a given day)? Rewrite as verifiable fact or as advice, or cut.
10. **Dated and decay-proof:** Are fast-changing facts (prices, opening months, market dates, free-tier limits) either verified and tied to a year or season, or generalised? Is `updated` set when facts changed?
11. **Service findable:** Can the reader find the practical part (steps, dates, what to pack, which export to choose) without rereading the story around it?
12. **Fair to competitors:** Would each competitor recognise its description as accurate and current on its German storefront? Is any claim worded more harshly than the facts support?
13. **Datenschutz no stronger than the facts:** Does every privacy statement stay within `en.js` (on the device, no account, no VoyageLog server)? No DSGVO, server-location or encryption claims.
14. **Headline keeps its promise (Ziffer 2):** Do title, description and H1 promise only what the body delivers? No „die besten“ if the piece only covers three apps without saying why.
15. **No invented people or quotes:** Are all examples clearly illustrative („ein Eintrag könnte so aussehen“) rather than presented as real users, real reviews or real testimonials? This is where generated text most often breaks Ziffer 2 and the AI clause of the preamble.
16. **Screens and names match the app:** Do described screens, buttons and feature names match `src/locales/de.js` today (Orte, Momente, Städtelisten, Best-of-Listen)?
17. **Plural byline holds:** Is it „wir“, the makers of VoyageLog, from first sentence to last FAQ answer, with no expert persona, no „ich“ and no implied individual reporter?
18. **Scope stated:** Does the piece say what it does not cover where that matters (only iPhone and Android, only apps available in the German-language stores, differences for Austria or Switzerland)?
19. **Correction recorded (Ziffer 3):** If the edit fixes a fact that was live and wrong, does the report say so plainly under "Removed or generalized", and does "Still uncertain, for the owner" flag a material error (a wrong competitor claim) so the owner can decide on a visible correction?

## What we never borrow

- Their text, in any length beyond a few words, and their headlines or headline patterns.
- Their list selections, rankings, "best of" choices, numbers of entries or the order of items. Our selection is made from our own criteria.
- Their signature formats and named rubrics (recurring column names, branded list or box formats, issue structures).
- Recognisable phrasing, slogans or mottos, including Warentest's grading vocabulary („Testsieger“, „gut (2,1)“, „Qualitätsurteil“) and seal or logo language.
- Their authority: we do not claim tests, lab results, on-site research or a named reporter.
- Their names inside articles: no mention, quote, comparison („wie bei Stiftung Warentest“) or link. They appear only in this file and in desk reports.
